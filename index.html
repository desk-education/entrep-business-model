1. What Is a Business Model?

A business model is a company's core plan for how it creates, delivers, and captures value while earning a profit.

It identifies the products or services a business plans to sell, its target market, and anticipated expenses. It helps a business plan how to generate profit and can help attract investment, recruit talent, and guide management and staff.

Businesses should review and update their models regularly so they can respond to trends, challenges, changing business environments, and market demand.

Remember: A business model should explain what the business offers, who it serves, why customers may choose it, what it needs to operate, and how it earns money.

2. Understanding Business Models

A business model is a high-level plan for operating a business profitably in a specific marketplace. A major component is the value proposition: a description of the goods or services offered and why they are desirable to customers, ideally showing how they differ from competitors.

A business model may also consider startup costs, financing sources, target customers, marketing strategy, competitors, revenue and expense projections, and potential business partners.

3. Key Elements of a Business Model

Element 1

Value Proposition

The product or service offered and why it is attractive to customers.

Ask: What customer need does it address?

Element 2

Target Market

The specific group of customers or clients the business intends to serve.

Ask: Who is most likely to buy it?

Element 3

Revenue Streams

How the business makes money, such as direct sales, subscriptions, or advertising.

Ask: Where does the money come from?

Element 4

Cost Structure

The major expenses required to run operations.

Ask: What must the business pay for?

Element 5

Key Resources & Activities

The assets, partnerships, and daily actions needed to make the model work.

Ask: What resources and work are essential?

Profit formula

Profit = Revenue − Total Costs
For example, if revenue is ₱1,000 and total costs are ₱650, the resulting profit is ₱350, before any unlisted expenses or taxes.

4. Types of Business Models

There is no single business model for every company. Businesses may use different models and may combine more than one approach. Select a model below to read its description.

Retailer

Buys finished goods from manufacturers or distributors and sells them to customers.

Manufacturer

Sources raw materials and uses labor, machinery, and equipment to produce finished goods. Example in the source: Ford Motor Company.

Fee-for-Service

Charges for labor or services, using an hourly rate or a fixed price. Example in the source: car wash.

Subscription

Charges ongoing payments for continued access or delivery. Example in the source: Spotify.

Freemium

Offers a basic, limited-scope product for free and attempts to convert users to a paid premium offering. Example: LinkedIn/LinkedIn Premium.

Bundling

Sells multiple products together, sometimes with a discount, to increase purchases from a customer.

Marketplace

Hosts a platform that helps buyers and sellers transact, often earning compensation. Examples: Shopee and Lazada.

Affiliate

Promotes a company's product through another person or platform, with compensation that may be fixed or based on sales. Example: social media influencers.

Razor Blade

Sells a durable primary product cheaply to encourage repeat purchases of a disposable or companion product. Example in the source: HP printers and ink.

Reverse Razor Blade

Sells a high-margin primary product upfront, then offers low-cost or free companion products. Example in the source: Apple iPhones and applications.

Franchise

Lets an operator use an established brand and business system at another location. Example in the source: Domino's Pizza.

Pay-as-You-Go

Charges based on how much of a product or service is used. Example in the source: utility companies.

Brokerage

Connects buyers and sellers without directly selling the good and may earn a percentage when a deal is completed.

Fast fact: A business may use multiple models for the same product. The source gives Spotify as an example of a service with both free and premium versions.
What is “tying” in the razor-blade discussion?

The source describes “tying” as an illegal strategy requiring the purchase of an unrelated good before a customer can buy another product. The example given is requiring customers to buy lotion before they can purchase disposable razor blades.

5. How to Create a Business Model

There is no “one size fits all” approach. The information sheet presents these seven broad steps:

  1. Identify your audience. Define the audience and target market you want to reach.
  2. Define the problem. Identify the customer need or problem your business intends to address.
  3. Understand your offerings. Decide what products or services you can provide and how they fit market needs.
  4. Document your needs. List the resources, operational requirements, and challenges involved in launching the business.
  5. Find key partners. Identify suppliers and other partners that can help the business succeed.
  6. Set monetization solutions. Decide how the business will earn money and work toward profit.
  7. Test your model. Use surveys, soft launches, pricing feedback, reviews, and customer responses to evaluate and improve the plan.
Think like an entrepreneur: A business idea should be tested with potential customers. Feedback can reveal whether the offering and price meet market needs.
